Reading for Credit Trouble: A Pre-Registered Test of Language-Model Filing Analysis as a Downgrade Early Warning

Rodrigo Madriz, ContextQuant (info@contextquant.com)

Working paper draft, 22 November 2026. Internal draft: numbers are taken from the program's results files as of this date; nothing here has been published.

Abstract

We test, in a pre-registered program in which every test ran once, whether a large language model's reading of corporate filings gives early warning of credit-rating downgrades beyond the information credit analysts already hold. Filings of 886 US-listed companies in five universes are read by the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, by FinBERT and by a large language model (Claude Haiku), and every verdict comes from filings made between January 2021 and June 2026. A reading of whether a filing is worse than the company's previous one (the Change Signal) places 23.4% of its worst tenth of filings in a downgrade within twelve months, against a base rate of 7.2% (lift 3.3, 90% interval 2.4 to 4.3), and 16.9% against 4.2% among companies whose S&P outlook was stable or positive. Its coefficient survives rating level, history and outlook, market and accounting variables, Merton distance to default, Altman Z, Ohlson O, 23 volatility measures, fundamental volatility, 13 macro series and the Lazy Prices and Brown-Tucker text-change measures (+0.769 log odds per standard deviation, odds ratio 2.16), and it keeps an independent coefficient in a horse race with every rival. A measurement-mode index built only from verified quotes (the Credit Stress Index), and the quarter-on-quarter change in language-model tone (Tone Shift), add smaller but significant amounts. Dictionary and FinBERT sentiment, as levels or changes, add nothing. Against matched companies of the same rating notch over the same window, flags are far more frequent before S&P downgrades (Change Signal 67.6% against 27.9%). The readings do not make a better rating model: controls alone reach an out-of-sample AUC of 0.875 and text adds +0.004, not significant; their value is the concentration of downgrades among flagged names. Name blanking, a recall probe used as a control (which removes 4% to 10% of the credit effects) and a test of the model's stated knowledge leave the credit results intact, and scores for filings made after the model's training cutoff are locked by cryptographic hash ahead of a prospective test.

1. Introduction

Credit analysts read filings, but they cannot read every filing of every issuer every quarter, and rating agencies change ratings slowly. A sizeable literature shows that disclosure text carries information about credit risk (Mayew, Sethuraman and Venkatachalam 2015; Donovan, Jennings, Koharki and Lee 2021) and that changes in filings matter (Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen 2020). Large language models (LLMs) now read text with something close to an analyst's judgement, which raises an obvious question: can a model that reads each new filing against the last one flag the companies that are about to be downgraded, earlier or more reliably than the information a credit desk already uses?

Two problems make evidence on LLMs in finance hard to read. The first is the familiar one of specification search: many signals, outcomes, horizons and subsamples, with the reported result chosen after the fact (Harvey, Liu and Zhu 2016; Hou, Xue and Zhang 2020). The second is specific to LLMs: a model trained on text published after a filing may simply remember how the company fared (Glasserman and Lin 2023; Sarkar and Vafa 2024; Lopez-Lira, Tang and Zhu 2025). A credible test has to answer both, and it has to hold the text to the baselines a credit professional would actually use.

This paper reports such a test. The program is pre-registered: hypotheses, outcomes, directions, controls and thresholds were written down, committed to version control and tagged before any test, extensions were added as dated amendments, and each registered test ran once. It covers five universes and 886 companies, with all verdicts out of sample (filings from 2021-01-01 to 2026-06-30). The credit question is held to agency information (rating level, history, outlook and watch), market risk (a battery of 23 volatility and drawdown measures), structural and accounting credit scores (distance to default, Altman Z, Ohlson O), fundamental volatility, macro conditions, and the published text-change measures. Every LLM result is then tested for memory, and filings written after the model's training data end are scored and locked before their outcomes exist.

Our contributions are four.

  1. A pre-registered, out-of-sample estimate of what an LLM reading of filing deterioration adds to downgrade prediction beyond agency, market, accounting and structural credit information and beyond published text measures. Three readings pass: the Change Signal (an LLM judgement of whether a filing is worse than the previous one, given the whole filing, five quarters of financial statements, the previous filing's MD&A and risk factors and the latest call), a measurement-mode Credit Stress Index built only from verified quotes, and Tone Shift (the quarter-on-quarter worsening of LLM tone). The Change Signal is the strongest and keeps an independent coefficient in a horse race with every rival.
  2. Evidence that standard filing sentiment adds nothing to a credit model. Dictionary category shares, dictionary and FinBERT net tone as levels (raw, against industry, against competitors) and as changes are tested head to head; none passes, and the six levels together add +0.0004 to out-of-sample AUC.
  3. Memory tests as validation of the credit results. Name blanking, a recall probe used as a control, a split by the model's stated knowledge and its formal interaction, a repeat-noise check with averaging, and locked post-cutoff samples. The credit results keep their size and significance under each of them.
  4. Rule-based evidence fixed before results. Decile tables at three horizons, a full quarter's flag list, the signal path before downgrades, and a comparison of each downgrade with matched companies of the same notch over the same window, so that no example and no chance baseline is chosen after the fact.

The results are also bounded. The readings do not improve the accuracy of a full credit model: the controls reach an out-of-sample AUC of 0.875, and no text signal adds a significant amount. Among calm companies on 2024 test data no reading improves AUC or the top-tenth hit rate after correction. When S&P had already placed a company on negative outlook or watch, the readings flagged first in only 25% to 48% of the subsequent downgrades. The value we find is of a different kind: downgrades are concentrated in the flagged tenth, the association survives every control, and before downgrades the flags appear far more often than at matched companies that were not downgraded, including before downgrades that came with no agency warning. Whether this holds on filings the model cannot have seen is the subject of a locked prospective test.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature. Sections 3 to 5 describe the data, the text measures and the design. Section 6 reports the credit results and Section 7 the memory tests. Section 8 states the limitations, Section 9 the extensions in progress and Section 10 concludes.

2. Related literature

2.1 Text and returns

The modern finance text literature starts from the observation that general-purpose word lists misclassify financial language; Loughran and McDonald (2011) build finance-specific lists that remain the standard dictionary baseline. Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen (2020) show that firms whose annual and quarterly reports change from one year to the next subsequently underperform, and that such changes predict news, earnings and bankruptcies; their cosine and Jaccard similarity measures ("Lazy Prices") are rivals in our credit models. Brown and Tucker (2011) measure year-over-year MD&A modification, and Feldman, Govindaraj, Livnat and Segal (2010) show that changes in MD&A tone carry information for returns. Li (2010), Davis, Piger and Sedor (2012) and Druz, Petzev, Wagner and Zeckhauser (2020) find that tone in filings, press releases and calls is associated with future earnings and with forecast revisions.

2.2 Large language models in finance

Lopez-Lira and Tang (2023) report that ChatGPT's reading of news headlines forecasts next-day returns; Kim, Muhn and Nikolaev (2024) find that an LLM given anonymised financial statements predicts the direction of earnings changes; Chen, Kelly and Xiu (2022) use LLM embeddings to forecast returns. For credit, Drinkall, Pierrehumbert and Zohren (2024) find that generative LLMs lose to traditional models at forecasting rating levels. That result frames ours: we do not ask an LLM to forecast a rating, and we find no gain in overall accuracy, but we do find an incremental association with downgrades that standard text methods do not deliver.

2.3 Look-ahead bias and memory

An LLM's training data may include the outcomes being predicted. Glasserman and Lin (2023) and Sarkar and Vafa (2024) set out the problem and propose anonymisation and point-in-time checks; Lopez-Lira, Tang and Zhu (2025) show that LLMs can reproduce historical financial data they have memorised. Gao, Jiang and Yan (2025) propose a date-only recall probe, close to the one we use, and attribute about 37% of a one-day return effect to it. He, Lv, Manela and Wu (2025) train chronologically consistent models that see only text available at each date. Our memory tests (Section 7) follow this literature: blanking, a probe used as a control, the knowledge interaction of Gao, Jiang and Yan, and post-cutoff samples.

2.4 Text and credit risk

Mayew, Sethuraman and Venkatachalam (2015) show that MD&A disclosure predicts the ability to continue as a going concern; Bonsall and Miller (2017) relate readability to bond ratings; Donovan, Jennings, Koharki and Lee (2021) build a machine-learning measure of credit risk from conference calls and MD&A; Mamaysky, Shen and Wu (working paper) find credit information in earnings calls priced in CDS spreads. Two recent papers appear close to ours but have been seen only as title listings and are unverified: Khoja (2024, SSRN 4965227), on LLM scores from calls and rating migration, and Fang, Hu, Tang, Wallis and Yang (SSRN 7429342, "Do Language Models Know Who Fails?"). Any claim of priority in this paper is conditional on reading both.

2.5 Text and volatility

That tone and risk language predict volatility is well established: Kogan, Levin, Routledge, Sagi and Smith (2009) predict volatility from 10-K text, Loughran and McDonald (2011) link negative and uncertain words to later volatility, Kravet and Muslu (2013) relate risk disclosure to risk perceptions, and Campbell, Chen, Dhaliwal, Lu and Steele (2014) show that risk-factor disclosures are informative. This literature is why the downgrade models hold a full volatility battery fixed (Section 5.2).

2.6 Earnings-call tone

Price, Doran, Peterson and Bliss (2012) find that call tone, especially in the question-and-answer section, predicts returns; Davis and Tama-Sweet (2012) show that press releases sound more upbeat than the MD&A and that a gloomier MD&A predicts worse performance; Davis, Ge, Matsumoto and Zhang (2015) find a manager-specific component in call tone. Our filing readings include the latest call as context for the Change Signal (Section 4.4).

2.7 Pre-registration and replication

Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) and Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020) document how many published return predictors fail out of sample or under stricter hurdles. Pre-registration is rare in archival finance and accounting; Bloomfield, Rennekamp and Steenhoven (2018) study the registered-report experiment at the Journal of Accounting Research. Our design (time-stamped registrations, single runs, family-wise correction, publication of every verdict) is a direct response to these critiques.

2.8 Position of this paper

That text predicts credit events is known. To our knowledge, and subject to reading the two unverified papers above, no paper combines an LLM reading of the direction of filing change, a full agency, market, structural and accounting control set, published text-change measures as rivals, a head-to-head with the dictionary and FinBERT, pre-registration, and memory tests. Relative to Lazy Prices, the Change Signal measures the direction of change rather than its amount, and it survives their measures as controls.

3.3 Credit ratings

Rating histories are the issuer credit rating histories that S&P, Moody's and Fitch publish under SEC Rule 17g-7(b), downloaded manually under each agency's terms and parsed into one table. Each company is matched by a single issuer rule: the parent's issuer rating, or, where the parent has none at that agency, its main debt-issuing subsidiary, with the reason recorded. The histories are published about twelve months late and end on 2025-09-30, so a twelve-month outcome is defined only for filings made by about September 2024. The main outcome, down_12, is a downgrade by any of the three agencies within twelve months of the filing date; 3- and 6-month horizons are secondary. Only the S&P rows carry outlooks and CreditWatch placements, so every "calm" or "warning" definition uses the S&P issuer rating state. Out of sample, rows with a twelve-month outcome number 9,564 rated filings in the all-studies pool (595 downgraded within twelve months), 8,186 in the Nasdaq-100 and semiconductor pool (538) and 2,834 in the Dow 30 and semiconductor pool on which the Change Signal is scored (138).

3.4 Prices, listing and bankruptcy rules

Every return in every registered test is computed from daily closing prices, compounded daily on the QQQ trading calendar, from a corrected panel (prices_a1, 4,503,951 rows, frozen with SHA-256 a41b1ae3...436a) built under Amendment 1. Its rules: bankruptcy and delisting events come from each company's own filings (8-K item 1.03, a text search of every 8-K for "chapter 11" and "voluntary petition", Form 15, Form 25 and plan effective dates), and the old shares' series ends at cancellation; returns are used only while the security trades on a national exchange (from Form 25, Form 8-A12B and periodic reports), so over-the-counter periods are excluded; any day priced under USD 0.01 is excluded. A monthly gate (no month above +100% or below -90%) and a daily gate (no day above +100% or below -75%) pass a move only with a filed event or an independent second source within 20%. On the final panel both gates show zero unexplained days and months; 13 days and 15 months were excluded; all 62 falls of 90% or more are explained; the wider bankruptcy search found two new cases (Mallinckrodt 2023, Spirit 2025). Market values use shares outstanding from SEC XBRL as filed, with a vendor source second (79 entities have no share count from either).

3.6 Analyst coverage and macro series

Analyst counts and consensus estimates come from a commercial vendor's historical records. Coverage differs sharply across universes: in the Nasdaq-100 universe 34.5% of companies have 11 or fewer analysts and 13% have no estimate record, while more than half of the Dow 30 universe has 19 or more. Thirteen macro series come from FRED as usable at each filing date (with publication lags): VIX, 2- and 10-year Treasury yields and their spread, investment-grade and high-yield option-adjusted spreads, Moody's Baa minus the 10-year yield (standing in for the BBB spread, because FRED keeps only three years of that series), the federal funds rate, real GDP growth, unemployment, CPI inflation and industrial production.

3.7 Periods

Methods were designed on 2015 to 2020 data. Every verdict reported here uses filings made between 2021-01-01 and 2026-06-30 ("out of sample"). Filings made from 2026-07-01 onward, after the published end of the LLM's training data, form the locked prospective sample (Section 7.5).

4. Text measures

Every reading is computed for each filing and, where stated, made relative to the company's industry group and to its named competitor group in the same quarter (the company's value minus the group median). "Worsening" signals are changes against the company's own previous comparable filing, signed so that a positive value means worse. Table 4 summarises the measures. Prompts, item definitions, the Credit Stress Index formula and the composition of the reading packages are proprietary and not reported; we describe the concepts, inputs and settings that a referee needs to judge the design. This is an internal draft, so we name the model family: Claude Haiku, version 5.5 (model identifier claude-haiku-5-5, published training-data end June 2026) for every new score from 2026-10-31. Older Haiku 4.5 scores are kept as the historical record and enter only the tests registered for them.

4.1 Dictionary

Net tone (positive minus negative words over total words) and the negative, uncertainty, constraining and litigious category shares of the MD&A, from the program's frozen Loughran-McDonald (2011) word lists, which are an abridged version of the full dictionary. The dictionary cannot remember outcomes.

4.2 FinBERT

Net tone from a financial-domain BERT model (the ProsusAI FinBERT release) applied to text chunks and averaged. It was trained before the out-of-sample period and is treated as memory-free.

4.3 Section-level LLM tone and Tone Shift

Each MD&A and risk-factor section is scored by the LLM with a frozen prompt (fixed in March 2026) for net sentiment, management confidence, risk tone and the forward-looking ratio. A "combined" signal blends the dictionary and LLM net tone with weights fixed in an earlier registration. Tone Shift (registry code F8) is the quarter-on-quarter worsening of LLM net tone against the company's own previous filing. Its registered home is the Nasdaq-100 universe; it is also tested pooled.

4.4 Change Signal

The Change Signal (registry code F9) comes from a "next-generation" reading registered in the November 2026 program (Part H). For each filing the model receives, using only what was public on the filing date: the full filing text (every item, untruncated, plus any exhibit holding the MD&A or annual report); the financial statements as numbers for the period and the four prior periods from XBRL as filed; the MD&A and risk factors of the previous comparable filing; and the transcript of the most recent earnings call held on or before the filing date. Company and period are given, not blanked. The model returns a structured set of fields, each with a short evidence quote. The Change Signal is minus the field that rates how much more positive or negative the filing is than the previous one, so that higher means worse. It was scored on the Dow 30 and semiconductor universes with their competitors (3,470 filings at a cost of USD 121.76), which gives 1,766 rated out-of-sample filings of 139 companies in the downgrade models ("pool H").

4.5 Credit Stress Index

The Credit Stress Index (CSI) is a measurement reading designed to limit what the model can contribute beyond the text. The model is asked only to list credit-relevant disclosures of predefined kinds (for example liquidity and financing language, risk factors added or removed since the previous filing, and the direction of guidance), each with an exact quote, and is told not to use knowledge beyond the text and not to rank or select. Each quote is matched to its source after normalising whitespace, quotation marks, dashes and case, and unmatched items are dropped: 96% of items verify on normal text (3.7% to 4.0% dropped), and every unmatched item in a hand-checked dry run of 285 items was a genuine non-match. The index is computed outside the model by a fixed, equally weighted formula of log verified counts, z-scored within year-quarter, set in the registration before any outcome was read. A reading costs about USD 0.0015 to 0.002 per filing. From Amendment 9 the registered CSI for prospective tests is the average of three identical readings (Section 7.4).

5. Research design

5.1 Registration, amendments and the single-run rule

The credit tests reported here belong to the November 2026 program registration (PREREGISTRATION.md, registered 2026-11-04T13:49:05Z, tag prereg-program-2026-11) and its nine dated amendments, each committed and tagged before any test it defines had run (Appendix A). Two study registrations of 31 October 2026 (semiconductors and the ratings study) and the Nasdaq-100 registration of 4 November 2026 precede it. Each amendment states why it was added, the exact tests, directions (one-sided or two-sided), the family for multiple-testing correction, reading rules fixed in advance, and an API budget with a worst-case quote from exact token counts. Every registered test ran once; a dry run with shuffled outcomes or signals preceded each part to check code, without producing a real result. Anything added after results were seen is labelled exploratory and carries no verdict. Deviations are listed in the run reports; the material ones are noted where they arise.

Verdicts are mechanical: PASS (expected sign, p below 0.05 after correction), CONSISTENT NOT SIGNIFICANT (expected sign, not significant), FAIL (wrong sign, or the expected sign not reached), DIFFERENT and NO DIFFERENCE FOUND for two-sided tests, and NOT TESTABLE or NOT ESTIMABLE (too few rows or events, or a separated logit).

5.2 Models and controls

Downgrade models are logits of down_12 (and of 3- and 6-month downgrades) on the standardised signal and controls, with year-quarter and Fama-French 12 industry effects; estimates are log odds per standard deviation of the signal. 5.3 Statistics

Intervals. 90% percentile intervals from a bootstrap clustered by company, 2,000 resamples, fixed seed 20261031. Multiple testing. Benjamini-Hochberg within each registered family or part; family sizes range from 2 to 2,992 tests. Permutation. In Amendment 3 a result counts as "signal" only if it survives the controls, a permutation test (the signal shuffled across companies within each year-quarter, 1,000 times, with the score statistic) and both comparison groups; likelihood-ratio tests compare models with and without the signal. Fama-MacBeth. For the program's market outcomes, cross-sectional regressions by period with Newey-West standard errors (lags fixed in the registration, with longer lags as a registered check). Out-of-sample AUC. Two designs: the J2 model fitted on 2015 to 2020 and tested on 2021 to 2026-06 (for signals scored in both periods), and, for signals scored only out of sample, a fit on 2021 to 2023 and a test on 2024 to 2026-06 filings with a full outcome window. Rule-based evidence. Decile tables, a full quarter's flag list and the signal path before downgrades were fixed in Amendment 7 before any result, so that no example could be chosen after the fact. Matched baselines. Amendment 9 compares each downgrade's 365-day window with up to ten windows of the same length, ending on the same date, at companies in the same pool and S&P notch that were not downgraded in the window or the following 90 days.

5.4 Power and budget

Each part reported its minimum detectable effect at 80% power where registered. All LLM requests ran through the Batch API under hard caps approved in advance (USD 200 for the first round, USD 90 for Amendment 5, USD 29 for Amendments 7 and 8, USD 20 from 2026-11-22); the November program cost about USD 236 in all.

6. Results

6.1 Downgrades: decile tables at 3, 6 and 12 months

The program's first credit tests (Part E of the registration) found the right direction for every tone-deterioration measure, with the year-on-year change strongest (AUC 0.57 to 0.59), and only 2 of 24 tests passing beyond rating level, prior downgrades and the past year's return. The Part H reading changed the picture: its change field reached a downgrade AUC of 0.70 to 0.74. Amendment 3 then put every signal through the J2 control set and permutation tests. The dictionary and FinBERT changes point the right way but do not survive (odds ratios 1.05 to 1.13 per standard deviation, BH p 0.05 to 0.30); LLM tone worsening in the Nasdaq-100 and semiconductor pool passes (odds ratio 1.17, likelihood-ratio BH p 0.013, permutation 0.016); and the Change Signal passes with an odds ratio of 2.19 (permutation p 0.007).

Table 6 gives the registered decile evidence (Amendment 7, Part Y1). Each reading is ranked over the rated out-of-sample filings of its universe, and decile 10 (worst) is the flag. For the Change Signal the flagged tenth is downgraded within twelve months 23.4% of the time against a base rate of 7.2%, a lift of 3.3 (90% interval 2.4 to 4.3); one tenth of the filings carries a third of all downgrades in the pool. The lift is about the same at 3 and 6 months, so the signal is not only picking up imminent actions. The CSI's flagged tenth is downgraded at about twice the base rate at every horizon, and Tone Shift's at 1.3 to 1.4 times. Figure 1 shows the full Change Signal distribution: the risk is concentrated in deciles 9 and 10, and the best deciles are downgraded at about half the base rate.

7. Look-ahead bias and memory

Claude Haiku 5.5's published training data run to June 2026, after every outcome in the out-of-sample window. Every LLM result in Section 6 is therefore memory-exposed: the model may have read about later downgrades. We ran five kinds of test, each registered before it ran.

7.1 Name blanking

Blanking replaces company names, tickers, brands, people, addresses and exact dates with placeholders. For the credit readings (Amendment 4, every downgrade event plus three matched non-downgraded filings), blanking barely moved the results (Table 15), but the model still named the company in 79% of blanked Tone Shift sections and 98% of blanked Change Signal packages, which carry financial statements, a call and the previous filing. Blanking therefore shows that the names themselves do not drive the results; it cannot show that the model's knowledge of the company does not. The one clean check is Tone Shift on the 413 rows where the model could not name the company: the blanked odds ratio there is 1.19, the same as elsewhere.

7.3 Knowledge split and knowledge interaction

If recall drove a result, it should be stronger where the model says it knows more. For returns it is: the 180-day return IC is +0.033 in the low-knowledge half and +0.109 in the high-knowledge half, the pattern recall would produce. For credit it is not: Tone Shift +0.046 against +0.045, Change Signal +0.189 against +0.207 (Table 16 note). Amendment 7 then ran the formal interaction of Gao, Jiang and Yan (2025), the signal times the model's stated knowledge, in the downgrade logits with the full controls. All six interactions show no difference (estimates -0.051 to +0.102, BH p 0.60 to 1.00). The credit readings work about as well on companies the model says it knows little about. This is evidence against recall, not proof.

8. Limitations

  1. Scope. The readings were also evaluated on other outcomes registered in the same program. On stock returns the language-model association does not survive the recall control, and drawdown rankings add little beyond standard volatility models; because most quarterly filings are made on or after the release of the earnings they report, filing tone is also not treated here as an earnings forecast. The paper's claims are confined to credit.
  2. Memory is not ruled out. Every LLM credit result is memory-exposed under the registered rule. Blanking, the probe control, the knowledge split and interaction all point against recall as the driver of the credit results, but only the post-cutoff tests can settle it, and the downgrade version needs at least 30 post-cutoff downgrades (expected late 2027). The Change Signal, which reads full named packages, is the most exposed and has the weakest blanking check (the model identified 98% of blanked packages).
  3. Accuracy. Text does not improve the out-of-sample AUC of a full credit model, overall or among calm companies. The value claimed is concentration of downgrades in flagged names and association beyond controls, not a better rating model.
  4. Agency lead. When S&P was already watching, the readings usually did not flag first. The surprise-downgrade evidence for the Change Signal rests on 6 events.
  5. Scope of the Change Signal. It is scored on 139 rated, mostly large companies (Dow 30 and semiconductors with competitors). Its semiconductor estimate is unstable (35 downgrades, a fit that did not fully converge with the probe). Extension to the Nasdaq-100 universe has not been tested.
  6. Ratings data. Histories end on 2025-09-30, so twelve-month outcomes cover filings to about September 2024 and the 2024 test year is short. Outlooks and watches are available only for S&P, while the downgrade outcome uses any agency. Fallen angels are too rare to test.
  7. Single model family and run-to-run noise. All new readings come from one model version. Single readings are noisy (CSI repeat correlation about 0.69), the model accepts no temperature setting, and results based on single readings (all of Section 6) carry that noise; the three-run average applies only to the prospective sample.
  8. Decile cut-offs. The decile tables rank filings over the out-of-sample panel. A real-time user would set cut-offs from earlier data; the matched-baseline and full-quarter tests use the same worst-decile cut-offs.
  9. Small universes. The single-sector universes have little power; the financials downgrade logits are separated and not estimable, and most pooled results are dominated by the Nasdaq-100 universe (Appendix Tables B1 and B2).
  10. Measurement choices. The dictionary lists are an abridged version of Loughran and McDonald; Moody's Baa minus the 10-year yield stands in for the BBB spread; interest coverage is missing for 44% of rows and accounting scores for a quarter to a third (handled by median imputation with a missing flag); some ordinary words were blanked by mistake in the first blanking test, which removes context and makes identification harder, not easier.
  11. Proprietary elements. Prompts, item definitions and the CSI formula are not published here, which limits independent replication. They are available to research partners under agreement.
  12. Literature. Two potentially close papers (Khoja 2024; Fang et al.) are unverified, and our novelty statement is conditional on reading them.
  13. Authorship and review. The program was designed and run by one researcher with LLM assistance in coding and planning. The registrations, code and results files are version-controlled, but no external party has yet audited them.

9. Extensions in progress

Quarterly locked scoring. Each new quarter's filings are read with the frozen readings, and the scores are hashed and committed before any outcome exists, as was done for the 766 filings of July to October 2026 (Table 18). The first post-cutoff readouts are due from March 2027: the registered drawdown and return tests on the first business day on or after 2027-03-22, and the downgrade test of the averaged Credit Stress Index once at least 30 downgrades follow post-cutoff filings (expected late 2027). Re-scoring the downgrade sample with a chronologically consistent model (He, Lv, Manela and Wu 2025) would give a second memory-proof check.

Broader coverage. The Change Signal is so far scored on 139 rated, mostly large companies. The next registrations extend it to the Nasdaq-100 universe and to further indexes and sectors, and add Canadian issuers through SEDAR+ (the current sample holds only issuers that file on EDGAR).

Credit-market outcomes. Rating actions are slow and discrete. CDS and bond spreads would show whether the readings lead market prices of credit as well as agency actions; this needs a bond or CDS data source that the program does not yet hold.

Alternative-data proxies. Some product-market data are published before the results they foreshadow. We have mapped free sources to all 888 entities (3,240 entity-proxy rows), each matched by a test query on company name or identifier (Table 19). Wikipedia page views cover two thirds of the companies, and app-store ratings, government contracts, developer activity, clinical trials and product, drug and device recalls cover most of the companies to which they apply. No history has been collected and no proxy has been tested: the identifiers are candidate matches to be confirmed by hand, and the tests (outcomes, horizons, comparison groups) will be registered before any data are read.

10. Conclusion

An LLM reading of whether a filing is worse than the company's previous one carries information about future credit-rating downgrades that the standard credit toolkit, the published text-change measures and conventional sentiment do not. Its flagged tenth is downgraded at three to four times the base rate, among all rated companies and among those the agency still calls stable; its coefficient survives every control and the recall probe; and its flags are far more common before downgrades than at matched companies over the same windows. A measurement reading built only from verified quotes, and the quarter-on-quarter change in LLM tone, add smaller amounts. Dictionary and FinBERT sentiment add nothing. The readings are ranking inputs for a watch list, not a replacement for the rating model: they do not raise the accuracy of a full credit model and they rarely beat an agency that is already watching.

The design is what makes these statements testable: hypotheses and thresholds registered before the tests, single runs, out-of-sample verdicts, the full credit toolkit and the published text benchmarks as controls, matched chance baselines, and memory tests that a recall-driven result would fail. The final check is prospective. Scores for filings written after the model's training data end are locked, and their results will be reported whatever they show.
