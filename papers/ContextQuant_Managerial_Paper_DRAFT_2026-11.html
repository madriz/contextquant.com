ContextQuant managerial paper

Early Warning from What Companies Write

What twelve years of filings tell credit and investment teams

Rodrigo Madriz, ContextQuant (info@contextquant.com)

November 2026. Business companion to the working paper Reading for Credit Trouble: A Pre-Registered Test of Language-Model Filing Analysis as a Downgrade Early Warning.

1Executive summary

The problem. Credit and investment teams cannot read every filing every quarter, and rating agencies move slowly. Vendors now sell AI "text signals", but few are tested in a way a risk committee would accept.

What we did. We read the annual and quarterly reports and earnings calls of 886 US companies from 2015 to 2026, about 1.03 billion words, with a standard finance word list, with FinBERT and with a large language model, and tested what those readings predict. Every test was time-stamped before it ran and ran once (about 8,700 in all); every result here comes from filings made from January 2021 to June 2026, years the methods were never tuned on.

What we found. What companies write gives credit early warning. A language-model reading of whether a filing is worse than the company's previous one, the Change Signal, picks out the companies most likely to be downgraded, months ahead:

3.3x
Downgrade rate within 12 months of the tenth of filings the Change Signal flags (23.4% against 7.2% for all filings).
4.1x
The same among companies the agency still rated stable or positive (16.9% against 4.2%). The flagged tenth held 41% of their downgrades.
68% vs 28%
Later-downgraded companies with a Change Signal flag in the year before, against matched companies that were not downgraded.
2.5x
Odds of a downgrade per step of worsening, after the full credit toolkit. Standard sentiment adds nothing to a credit model; ours does.

The flag keeps its weight after the tools a credit analyst already uses: rating and outlook, market data, distance to default, Altman Z, Ohlson O, 23 volatility measures and published text-change measures. Companies later downgraded already read worse four quarters ahead, and in one full quarter fixed by rule, the warnings that came true arrived about seven months before the downgrade. A second reading, the Credit Stress Index, covers the whole panel at a fraction of a cent per filing and lists the sentences behind each score.

How to use it. As a watch-list input that concentrates downgrades, beside a rating model rather than instead of one. Most flagged names are not downgraded within a year, so a flag is a reason to read. It is strongest where the agency is still calm. Scores on filings made after the model's training cutoff are locked, with readouts from March 2027.

What we propose. Quarterly locked scoring of new filings, broader coverage including Canadian issuers, credit-market outcomes and alternative data (Section 9), built with asset managers, banks and wealth managers through a partnership and a pilot with success criteria fixed before it starts (Section 10).

2The study

ContextQuant reads what companies write about themselves and compares it with what they wrote last quarter and with what their competitors write. The study covers 886 US-listed companies in five universes (the Dow 30 with competitors, the Nasdaq-100 with competitors, semiconductors, banks and insurers, and healthcare), with their annual and quarterly reports and earnings calls from 2015 to 2026 (Section 8).

Readings. Every filing is read with the Loughran and McDonald finance word list, with FinBERT and with a large language model. Three language-model readings carry the credit results. We describe what they measure, not how they are built.

  • Change Signal: a reading of whether a filing is worse than the company's previous one, read in context. It was scored on the Dow 30 and semiconductor studies with their competitors: 1,766 rated filings from 139 companies.
  • Tone Shift: the quarter-on-quarter worsening of the tone of the management discussion and risk factors, across the Nasdaq-100 universe and its competitors.
  • Credit Stress Index: a measurement reading. The model lists credit-relevant disclosures, each with an exact quote that is checked against the filing (96% verify), and a fixed formula turns the verified items into an index. It costs a fraction of a cent per filing, works across the whole panel, and comes with the sentences behind each score. It is the average of three readings, which makes it repeatable (reliability 0.87).

Outcome and design. A filing is flagged when its reading is in the worst tenth. The outcome is a downgrade by any of the three major agencies within 3, 6 or 12 months of the filing date; S&P histories also give outlooks and CreditWatch placements. Methods were designed on 2015 to 2020; every result in this paper comes from filings made from January 2021 to June 2026, and ratings data run to September 2025. Every test was registered before it ran and ran once (Section 7).

One tenth of the filings carries a third of all the downgrades. The least worrying tenth, at the other end, is downgraded at about half the base rate.

3.2 Where the agency is calm

The most useful warnings are the ones the agency has not yet given. Among filings made while the company's S&P outlook was stable or positive and it was not on CreditWatch, the Change Signal's flagged tenth was downgraded within a year at 16.9%, against 4.2% for all such filings, and it held 41% of their downgrades. The pattern holds in the Dow 30 alone (4.0 times) and, more weakly, for the Credit Stress Index (2.0 times) and Tone Shift (1.9 times). Every one of these readings keeps a significant weight among calm companies when the full set of credit controls is in the model. The flag is not just repeating a warning the agency has already issued.

3.3 Before downgrades, against matched companies

A fair test must ask how often a company that was not downgraded would also have been flagged. A downgrade's year-long window holds three or four filings, so the chance that at least one is flagged is far above one in ten. So each downgrade is compared with up to ten companies at the same S&P rating notch, over the same 365 days, that were not downgraded.

The fair baseline is much higher than one in ten: a matched company had a flagged filing in the same window 28% (Change Signal), 41% (Tone Shift) and 28% (Credit Stress Index) of the time. Against it, every reading still flags downgrades more often. For downgrades that came with no agency warning, Tone Shift flagged 74% against 40% for matched companies. The Change Signal flagged all six of its surprise downgrades, but six cases are too few to quote as a rate; the Credit Stress Index flagged 42% against 29%, the right direction but not significant.

3.4 The signal path: months ahead

If the reading picks up genuine deterioration, it should worsen before the downgrade, not only at it. It does. Companies later downgraded already read worse than matched companies four quarters ahead, and the gap widens into the downgrade quarter.

3.6 Beyond the credit toolkit

A credit officer's real question is not "does tone go with downgrades" but "does it tell me anything my models do not". We put each reading into a downgrade model that already holds the rating level, rating history and outlook, the stock's return and volatility, size, leverage, analyst coverage, the last earnings surprise, market and sector returns, 13 macro series and industry and calendar effects, and then the standard credit toolkit: Merton distance to default, Altman Z, Ohlson O, 23 volatility measures (realised, range-based, EWMA and GARCH forecasts, beta, downside beta, idiosyncratic volatility, skewness, past drawdowns) and the volatility of profits, sales and cash flow.

In plain terms: after everything above is accounted for, each one-standard-deviation worsening of the Change Signal multiplies the odds of a downgrade within a year by about 2.5 in the Dow 30 and 2.2 with the semiconductor companies added. Adding the published text-change measures (Lazy Prices similarity and the Brown and Tucker modification score) changes almost nothing: the Change Signal stays at +0.80 to +0.93, Tone Shift at +0.13 to +0.14. The readings work as well for companies the model says it knows little about as for those it knows well (no difference in 6 of 6 tests), which is further evidence against memory.

3.7 Standard sentiment adds nothing; our readings do

We tested sentiment the way it is usually sold: word-list shares of negative, uncertain, constraining and litigious words and their changes, and word-list and FinBERT net tone as levels, raw and relative to industry and competitors. Beside a credit analyst's standard information, none of them helps predict a downgrade: all six tone levels together add +0.0004 to out-of-sample accuracy (AUC), on a scale where 1.0 is perfect. In a single model holding every reading and every rival text measure at once, two of our readings keep a clear weight and the standard measures have none. Our scope is company filings and rating downgrades; news and social-media sentiment products were not tested.

4How to use it

The readings are built for one job: deciding which names to look at first, and why. Six points set how to use them.

  • A watch-list input that concentrates downgrades. One tenth of filings carries a third of the downgrades (Table 2). About three in four flagged filings (Change Signal) to four in five (Credit Stress Index) are not followed by a downgrade within a year, so a flag is a reason to read, not a reason to act.
  • Beside a rating model, not instead of one. A model built on agency ratings, outlooks and market data already ranks downgrade risk well (out-of-sample AUC 0.875), and the readings do not measurably sharpen that overall ranking (+0.004). Their value is the concentration of downgrades in flagged names and information the model does not hold (Table 4).
  • Strongest where the agency is still calm. Among companies with a stable or positive outlook and no watch, the flagged tenth is downgraded at 4.1 times the base rate (Section 3.2). Where the agency already has a company on negative outlook or watch, its own warning usually comes first (our flag led in 25% to 48% of those cases), so the readings add most for names the agency has not yet flagged.
  • Know the coverage. The Change Signal, the strongest reading, covers 139 rated, mostly large companies in the Dow 30 and semiconductor studies with competitors. The Credit Stress Index and Tone Shift cover the wider panel. Outlook and watch data are from S&P; ratings data run to September 2025.
  • Read the sentences. Every Credit Stress Index item comes with a quote checked against the filing, and the index is the average of three readings, so the same filing gives a stable score.
  • The post-cutoff readout is pending. On historical data, controlling for what the model remembers removes only 4% to 10% of the credit effects, which all stay significant. The decisive test is on filings made after the model's training cutoff: those scores are locked (Section 7), with the first readout in March 2027 and the credit readout once at least 30 new downgrades exist, expected in late 2027.

5What is new

That text in filings carries information about credit risk is not new. Donovan, Jennings, Koharki and Lee (2021) built a credit-risk measure from calls and the management discussion, and Mayew, Sethuraman and Venkatachalam (2015) linked that discussion to going-concern risk. Cohen, Malloy and Nguyen (2020), in "Lazy Prices", showed that companies whose filings change are riskier. And when large language models were asked to forecast credit ratings directly, they lost to traditional models (Drinkall and co-authors, 2024).

What is likely new, to our knowledge, is the combination:

  • a language-model reading of whether a filing has deteriorated, which adds to downgrade prediction beyond agency, market, accounting, distance-to-default and published text measures;
  • run head to head with the word-list and FinBERT measures, which add nothing;
  • pre-registered, each test run once, with direct memory tests and a locked post-cutoff record.

The Change Signal also differs from Lazy Prices: it reads the direction of change, better or worse, not just its amount, and it survives their measures as controls. Our memory tests follow recent work on look-ahead bias in language models (Glasserman and Lin 2023; Sarkar and Vafa 2024; Lopez-Lira, Tang and Zhu 2025; Gao, Jiang and Yan 2025). Two recent working papers that may overlap with ours have not yet been reviewed in full; until they are, we make no claim to be first.

6Uses by stakeholder

Each use below rests on the same evidence: credit outcomes. In every case the reading is an input to a person's judgement, delivered with the company's own sentences.

Credit risk: banks, credit funds, insurers' investment teams

Decision
Which names go on, or come off, the watch list this quarter; which calm-rated names deserve a review before the agency moves; where to spend scarce analyst hours.
Evidence
Flagged tenth downgraded at 3.3 times the base rate, 4.1 times among calm companies with 41% of their downgrades (Table 2); 68% of later-downgraded companies flagged in the year before, against 28% of matched companies (Figure 2); weight beyond distance to default, Altman Z, Ohlson O and volatility (Table 4). Adds most where the agency has not yet warned (Section 4).
Delivery
A quarterly list after each filing season: every covered filing with its readings, the flag, and the verified quotes behind the Credit Stress Index. As a file or feed into the client's existing watch-list or surveillance system, time-stamped and locked before outcomes.
Workflow
  1. The list arrives shortly after each filing season closes; names in the client's book come first.
  2. The analyst filters to calm-rated names (stable or positive outlook, no watch), where the flag adds most.
  3. For each, the analyst reads the quoted passages, for example language on refinancing, liquidity or covenant headroom, and the change against the previous filing.
  4. Decision logged: add to watch, request a management call, tighten limits, or dismiss with a reason.
  5. Outcomes are recorded each quarter, building the client's own track record of the flag.

Covenant and refinancing language is something the quoted passages let an analyst find quickly; we have not tested sub-types of disclosure separately.

Equity research: heads of research, sector analysts

Decision
Which new filings to read first this week; which covered names need their risk section revisited; which names outside active coverage show deteriorating disclosure.
Evidence
The flags concentrate later credit deterioration (Tables 2 and 3) and the signal path shows it a year ahead (Figure 3). The evidence is about credit deterioration: the ranking is a reading list, not a share-price forecast.
Delivery
A triage ranking of each week's or quarter's filings by Change Signal and Credit Stress Index, each with the passages that triggered it and a link to the filing.
Workflow
  1. Monday: the analyst opens the ranking for their sector.
  2. The top flagged filings are read first, starting with the quoted passages.
  3. Findings feed the risk section of the investment case or a credit-side conversation with the fixed-income team.
  4. Thinly covered names in the universe get the same Credit Stress Index reading as large caps, so nothing is skipped for lack of an analyst.

Whether the flags work better or worse for thinly covered companies has not been tested; the extension program would test it.

Wealth management: CIO offices, advisors, suitability and risk teams

Decision
Which client holdings, in single-name bonds or shares, need a review conversation; which flagged holdings sit in conservative or income mandates; what to tell a client and why.
Evidence
The same credit evidence. Most useful where the agency is still calm (Section 3.2), which is where an advisor has no other warning. About three in four flags do not lead to a downgrade within a year, so the light means "review", not "sell".
Delivery
A risk light beside each covered holding (flagged this quarter or not), with two or three sentences in the company's own words and a plain-language note an advisor can use.
Workflow
  1. Each quarter the CIO office sees flagged holdings across the book, weighted by client exposure.
  2. Flags in conservative mandates go to the suitability review with the quoted passages attached.
  3. Advisors get talking points drawn from the company's own disclosure, not a model's opinion.
  4. The review and its outcome are documented; no trade is automated.

7How we test, and how to judge any text signal

A buyer cannot tell a real signal from a lucky one by looking at a back-test. What a buyer can check is the process. Ours has five parts.

  1. Pre-registration. Before any test ran, the hypothesis, the outcome, the threshold and the expected direction were written down and time-stamped in version control: four study registrations and nine dated amendments. Results cannot be quietly redefined after the fact.
  2. Single runs. Each of about 8,700 tests ran once. Results were corrected for the number of tests in each family, so a few lucky hits among many cannot pass as findings.
  3. Out of sample. Methods were designed on 2015 to 2020. Every result in this paper comes from filings made from January 2021 to June 2026.
  4. Timing and memory tests. Every outcome was checked against the date the filing became public, so no result uses a document published after the outcome. For memory we removed names from documents, asked the model what it remembered with no document and used that as a control, checked whether results depend on how well the model knows a company, and measured run-to-run noise.
  5. A locked post-cutoff record. Filings made after the model's training cutoff cannot be remembered. In November 2026 we scored 766 such filings (579 companies, filed 1 July to 30 October 2026), before any outcome existed, and committed SHA-256 fingerprints of the score files to a time-stamped repository. A fingerprint changes if a single digit in the file changes, so anyone can later verify the scores were not altered.

9Where this is going

The study has answered its first question: there is a credit early-warning signal in what companies write, and it holds under hard testing. The program now extends it in four directions.

Quarterly locked scoring of new filings

Each filing season is scored, fingerprinted and committed before any outcome exists, so the record grows on filings no model can have seen. The first batch (766 filings, July to October 2026) is locked and the November to December season is next. The first post-cutoff readout is in March 2027; the credit readout follows once at least 30 new downgrades exist, expected in late 2027. Each quarter's flags are then revisited at 3, 6 and 12 months, hits and misses alike, in the format of Table 3.

Broader coverage

More indexes and sectors, the Change Signal on every universe rather than 139 companies, and Canadian issuers through SEDAR+, the Canadian securities filing system.

Credit-market outcomes

Ratings change slowly by design. Credit default swap and bond spreads move every day, so they test whether the readings lead the market as well as the agencies. Each test will be registered before it runs.

Alternative data that can move before published results

Some evidence about a company's business shows up outside its filings before the numbers do: public attention, app adoption, government contract awards, developer activity, clinical-trial progress and product recalls. Research on consumer reviews has shown that customer opinion can lead reported results (Huang 2018). We have mapped free public sources to the 888 entities in the study. These are candidate matches from name searches, to be confirmed by hand; no history has been collected and nothing has been tested yet. Any test will be registered first.

10The partnership proposal

The next questions need partners: does the signal work on your names, in your process, on filings no model has seen?

The roadmap in Section 9 goes faster, and closer to real decisions, with partners. A partnership adds three things to it: delivery into the partner's own watch lists, surveillance tools or advisor platforms, with the quoted passages attached; the outcomes the partner cares about, such as internal ratings, other agencies' outlooks, spreads and loan events; and a pilot on the partner's own names.

A pilot with success criteria fixed in advance

A pilot is itself a test, and we would run it the way we run everything else: registered before it starts, run once, and reported whatever the result.

What partners provide

  • Data access: internal ratings, other agencies' outlook and watch histories, spreads or loan data where available.
  • A portfolio for the pilot: the holdings or watch list against which the flags are scored.
  • Practitioner review: credit officers and analysts who judge whether the flags and passages help, and tell us where they do not.

In return, partners get the readings and passages for their names, a say in what is tested next, and early access to each post-cutoff readout.

11Glossary

AUC (area under the curve)
A measure of how well a model ranks cases. 0.5 is a coin toss, 1.0 is perfect. A downgrade model using ratings and market data already reaches 0.875; our readings add +0.004.
Calm company
A company whose S&P outlook was stable or positive, and which was not on CreditWatch, on the filing date.
Flagged tenth
The tenth of filings with the worst reading.
Lift
How many times more often flagged filings are followed by a downgrade than filings in general. A lift of 3.3 means 23.4% against 7.2%.
Look-ahead bias
Using information that was not available at the time of the decision, for example a filing published after the outcome, or a language model that read about the outcome during training.
Matched control
A company at the same rating notch, over the same time window, that was not downgraded; used to judge how often a flag would appear by chance.
Odds ratio
How much the odds of a downgrade multiply for each one-standard-deviation worsening of a reading, after controls. An odds ratio of 2.5 means the odds are two and a half times higher. The log odds in Tables 4 and 5 are the same number on a log scale.
Out of sample
Data the method was not designed or tuned on. Here: filings made from January 2021 to June 2026.
Pre-registration
Writing down and time-stamping each hypothesis, outcome, threshold and expected direction before running the test, so results cannot be redefined afterwards.
Recall
The share of all downgrades that followed a flagged filing. A recall of 33% means one in three downgrades was preceded by a flag. (Distinct from the model's "recall" of training data, which we call memory.)
Recall probe (memory control)
Asking the language model, with no document, what happened to a company after a date, and using its answer as a control for what it remembers.
SHA-256 fingerprint
A 64-character code computed from a file. Any change to the file changes the code, so a published fingerprint proves the file has not been altered since.
